Chapter 13 · Spatial Intelligence for Intelligent Assistance

13.5Pivotal Task-3: Empowering Vulnerable Users

PDF p. 442· 3 min read

The highest-impact regime uses spatial intelligence to compensate for a sensory, cognitive, or motor impairment, acting as a prosthesis for the user’s perception or agency. The assistant translates the environment into a queryable, navigable, and actionable representation and delivers it through a channel the user can still use (audio, haptics, or physical action). Because the loop closes around a vulnerable person in their own home, safety, privacy, and personalization are design constraints from the start.

Assistance for the blind and visually impaired. For blind and low-vision users, spatial intelligence turns an unknown and hazardous environment into a queryable one. The oldest strand is sensory substitution, exemplified by the vOICe [2939], which sonifies the spatial structure of an image into sound. The modern strand is conversational visual question answering: VizWiz [2940] introduced a deployed system in which a blind user photographs their surroundings and asks a spoken question, and the VizWiz Grand Challenge [2941] turned the resulting real-world distribution, including blurry and unanswerable images, into the reference benchmark for assistive VQA. Indoor wayfinding is addressed by smartphone navigation aids such as NavCog [2942], which deliver turn-by-turn audio guidance from accurate beacon-based localization, and by three-dimensional spatial audio that places points of interest around the user. Commercial assistive applications include Microsoft’s Seeing AI for scene and text narration1, Be My Eyes for volunteer- and model-mediated visual assistance2, and wearable systems from OrCam3, Envision4, and biped.ai5 for scene description and obstacle warning. These tools move from passive obstacle warning to active interrogation, in which the user asks “is there a free seat?” or “read the sign on the door” and the assistant retrieves the answer.

Cognitive and mobility assistance. For users with cognitive decline or limited mobility, spatial assistants provide external scaffolding for the activities of daily living. Ambient assisted living systems such as the CASAS smart home [2943] instrument a dwelling to track the resident’s location and activity, learning the spatio-temporal patterns whose violation (a fall, a missed meal) signals a need to intervene; wearable accelerometry supplies the canonical fall-detection paradigm [2944].

Where monitoring gives way to physical action, the assistant becomes a shared-control effector. Smart wheelchairs [2945] and wheelchair-mounted assistive arms use collaborative control to enforce spatial safety zones, for example forbidding a chair from driving down a staircase, so that the user retains mobility without constant supervision; the goal-arbitration that underlies such control is the same shared-autonomy machinery introduced above, here cast as a decision-theoretic problem [2946]. Assistive feeding [2947] shows how fine spatial reasoning (where to acquire a bite, how to transfer it to the user’s mouth) becomes a caregiving act. The platform lineage runs from Robots for Humanity [2948], which let a man with quadriplegia perform self-care through a teleoperated PR2, to the affordable Stretch mobile manipulator [2949], with physics-based simulation of caregiving tasks consolidated in Assistive Gym [2950]. Commercial assistive hardware in this space includes the Kinova JACO arm6 and the Obi feeding device7. This regime overlaps the clinical setting of Section 12.1, but the boundary is clear: there the focus is diagnosis and surgical intervention, whereas here it is daily living in the home.
