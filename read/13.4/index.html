Chapter 13 · Spatial Intelligence for Intelligent Assistance

13.4Pivotal Task-2: Co-habiting and Collaborating

PDF p. 440· 3 min read

As the distance between assistant and human shrinks, spatial intelligence must become social intelligence. The assistant must model the human as a co-located agent with intent, viewpoint, and comfort zones, not as a moving obstacle or a bare command source, and act predictably and collaboratively. Running through every part of this task is the reconciliation of reference frames: the assistant’s own egocentric view, the human’s egocentric view, and a shared allocentric map must be kept aligned.

Socially-aware navigation. Moving through a populated space is not collision avoidance; it is adherence to the unwritten contracts of proxemics. The classical account is the social force model [2931], which casts pedestrian motion as attraction to a goal under repulsion from others, operationalizing Hall’s anthropological zones of personal space. Data-driven successors learn the interaction directly: Social LSTM [2233] couples per-person recurrent predictors through a social-pooling layer to forecast jointly plausible trajectories, and deep reinforcement-learning planners such as the attention-based crowd-robot model [2932] weigh neighbors to navigate dense crowds while avoiding the “freezing robot” failure that purely reactive planners suffer [2933]. Progress is measured on human-demonstration corpora such as SCAND [2934], which records hours of socially compliant teleoperation for imitation and inverse reinforcement learning. The trend is toward anticipatory planning, in which the assistant infers the human’s intended path and yields proactively, and toward learnable cost functions that adapt passing distances to individual or cultural preference rather than enforcing one universal margin.

Grounded instruction following and spatial dialogue. A collaborating assistant must resolve spatial language against what it sees, including the deictic references (“put it next to the other bowl,” “the one over there”) that pervade human speech. Vision-and-language navigation, introduced with the Room-to-Room benchmark [1403], established the problem of following natural-language route instructions in photorealistic buildings; ALFRED [1296] extended it to long-horizon household tasks with irreversible state changes, and TEACh [1297] cast the task as a two-agent dialogue in which a commander and follower must build shared spatial reference over multiple turns. Resolving a referring expression to a specific object in a cluttered 3D scene is studied directly by ScanRefer [365] and ReferIt3D [366], whose same-class distractors force the model to rely on spatial relations rather than appearance. These benchmarks build on the reasoning capabilities of Chapter 7; the assistant requirement is to maintain a spatial working memory that links current observations to earlier instructions so that deixis can be resolved.

Shared autonomy and collaboration. When full autonomy is brittle, the assistant acts as a third hand under shared control. The defining formulation is shared autonomy via hindsight optimization [2935], in which the robot infers a distribution over the user’s spatial goal and assists toward it without committing to a single confident prediction, formalizing the earlier policy-blending account of arbitration between human and robot intent [2936]. Equally important is making the assistant’s own motion readable: legible motion [2616] shapes a reaching trajectory so a human can quickly infer its intended target, which is what allows close-quarters collaboration to feel safe. Physical handovers, where giver and receiver must coordinate in space and time [2937], are the elementary collaborative act on which assistive manipulation is built.

Perspective-taking and egocentric grounding. Many assistants observe the world from a wearable, first-person viewpoint, so the frame-alignment problem becomes explicit. Ego4D [673] and its successors anchor egocentric perception at scale, and dedicated capture platforms such as Project Aria1 supply the calibrated multi-sensor first-person stream such assistants run on, while spatial-VQA backbones such as SpatialVLM [145] give a vision-language model the quantitative 3D reasoning (distances, sizes, directions) needed to answer spatial questions about the user’s scene. The hardware and pose-estimation side of wearable sensing is treated in Section 9.3, and the augmented-reality runtime that renders guidance into the user’s view is treated in Section 10.1; here the contribution is the reasoning that converts a first-person observation into a collaborative action in the human’s frame. Embodied-AR applications make this alignment problem explicit when directions, warnings, or manipulation cues must be registered into the user’s view [2938].
